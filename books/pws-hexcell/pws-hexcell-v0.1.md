# Purpose of this edition

This book reconstructs PWS HexCell as a physical object, a manufacturing experiment and a bounded wall test. It is not a catalog entry and not a promotional description of an unbuilt house. Every important mechanism is followed from feedstock identity through mould filling, warm core release, cooling, inspection, course assembly, wall testing and final disassembly. Unknown dimensions remain unknown; they are converted into explicit measurements or freeze decisions rather than filled with editorial precision.

The controlling design is intentionally plain: one rectangular block with full side faces, an open vertical field of hexagonal voids, two basic lower cubic locators with matching upper pockets, and staggered brick-like courses. Side hooks, decorative grooves, special corner blocks, vacuum electronics and several parallel block sizes are not part of V1. They may return only if a measured wall problem proves that the simple geometry cannot solve it.

**Iteration record.** Working revision 0.1 is the first English book consolidation, not the first attempt at the invention. PWS passed through many informal branches before the surviving technical record. The exact attempt count cannot be reconstructed honestly; the book therefore records the current correction and the surviving evidence instead of inventing a linear revision history.

PWS HexCell is the smallest honest production nucleus of the larger Plastique Wall System. Its full outer sides create a handleable block and protect the internal web field. The open vertical voids reduce material and provide visible internal paths. Two shallow lower forms locate the next course without pretending to be a structural snap-lock. Staggered rows provide the first lateral organisation. The V1 goal is not maximum insulation or maximum novelty; it is one repeatable block whose failures remain observable.

## 2\. The problem the invention addresses

Conventional wall construction is often wet, permanent and destructive to service. Layers are bonded, rendered or closed so that a later cable, leak, damaged insulation layer or material-recovery operation requires breaking finished work. The wider PWS family asks whether a wall can behave more like a serviceable assembly: courses can be positioned dry, layers remain separately replaceable, internal routes stay inspectable and the material can return to a known stream after disassembly.

The second problem is material credibility. Local waste plastic may be abundant, but mixed waste is not automatically a building material. A useful system must turn one known, cleaned and documented polymer family into a traceable part, reveal contamination and incomplete fusion, preserve rejected samples and connect each block to its process history. HexCell deliberately makes these failures visible before an expensive monolithic tool or inhabited building hides them.

## 5\. What the physical print proves

The green print establishes the tactile proposition that a cellular part can still feel like a solid block. Its side faces are continuous rather than an exposed honeycomb mesh. The upper view shows a multi-row field of openings. The side and oblique photographs show a light hand-held object and small lower positioning forms. The print therefore justifies measurement and a moulding experiment; it does not justify copying dimensions from a separate seven-core diagram.

The first high-value action is measurement, not a new render. The existing object should be weighed and recorded in six orthogonal views with a scale. Length, width, height, void count and pattern, minimum web, side-wall thickness, locator position and upper-pocket geometry should be recovered. That single record would resolve more uncertainty than another unmeasured CAD branch.

## 7\. Locked functional architecture of V1

Although numerical dimensions remain open, the functional architecture is sufficiently constrained. The body is rectangular so it can be held, gauged and supported on simple flat references. Side faces remain full and free of undercuts. The hex field stays open from top to bottom. Two lower cubic or simple rectangular forms locate the course below; matching pockets remain visible and cleanable. Rows overlap like masonry so one vertical joint does not continue through the wall. A durable revision and orientation mark connects the part to material and mould records.

| Component | Function | V1 rule | First proof |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Full-sided body | Handling surface and protection of the internal webs | No side hooks or decorative undercuts | Edge inspection, drop, side squeeze and fracture map |
| Vertical hex field | Reduce volume, form multi-directional webs and create inspection paths | Open both ends; one frozen pattern | Probe every void, measure webs and section a sacrificial part |
| Two lower locators | Repeat course position | Shallow lead-in; no snap lock | Seating force, rocking, dust and ten cycles |
| Matching upper pockets | Receive the locators | Visible, cleanable and not a water trap | Gauge and fit across temperature cycles |
| Staggered courses | Interrupt aligned joints and organise the wall | Brick-like overlap before special corners | Six-block corner and short dry wall |
| Revision mark | Trace geometry and material | Readable without a network | Abrasion, cleaning and blind trace |

## 8\. Full side faces and load distribution

A full side face gives the hand, seal, guide or removable panel a continuous contact surface. It also distributes a local touch across several internal webs instead of exposing every cellular edge. That does not make the block structural. A thin side face can oil-can, crack over a void or peel from poorly fused material. Side thickness must therefore be frozen together with web thickness, corner radius, feedstock and cooling support—not selected from appearance alone.

The side face also controls manufacturing. A very thick face raises mass, cooling time and differential shrinkage. A very thin face fills poorly and can burn or tear during core release. The first tool should keep the outer surface simple enough that flash, cold joints and warpage can be photographed and measured without cosmetic texture. Handles, façade anchors and service clips belong to later independent interfaces, not to the V1 mould.

## 9\. The open vertical hexagonal void field

A regular hexagonal opening is not universally stronger than a circle or rectangle. Its value here is a repeatable tessellation that creates webs in several directions and leaves the interior visually inspectable. The field may reduce mass, provide drainage or future service paths and let the block be illuminated from one end during inspection. Performance still depends on minimum web, side-wall thickness, void-to-edge distance, material fusion, root radii and the direction of load.

Open channels have liabilities. Dirt may bridge a pocket, water may remain if the base detail blocks drainage, and a narrow web can appear complete while containing a cold joint. Every final opening must accept a simple straightness probe. The V1 layout should avoid blind cross passages, trapped inserts and undercuts so that a failed block can be sectioned and its formation understood.

## 10\. Course locators and staggered rows

The two lower forms are locators, not load-bearing studs. They help a block find a repeated position while the base, overlapping courses and any controlled vertical compression perform the larger assembly work. A tight interference fit would be hazardous because recycled polymers shrink, expand with temperature and collect dust. An excessively loose fit creates apparent alignment while the course can still slide. The correct clearance must be derived from measured production spread and assembly tests.

Each locator needs a robust lead-in without a fragile thin lip. The receiving pocket must be inspectable and should provide a route for dust or cleaning. A damaged locator is recorded and quarantined rather than carved by eye in the wall. If production requires several locator sizes, heating or hand filing to assemble one revision, V1 has failed its simplicity criterion.

# PART III · LAYERED MOULD AND MANUFACTURING CYCLE

## 13\. The layered experimental mould

The first useful hot tool is deliberately modular. Its cavity is assembled from flat, laser-cut steel layers rather than machined as one irreversible block. The lower package establishes the base and the core positions. Two side packages close the rectangular body. The upper package guides the long hexagonal cores and spreads clamp load. Individual cores pass through the cavity and are withdrawn separately. Alignment keys define the geometry; bolts supply compression. This separation allows one damaged or poorly dimensioned plate to be replaced without discarding the complete tool.

A layered tool is not automatically cheap or accurate. Burrs, plate flatness, accumulated thickness error and thermal distortion can create a stepped cavity or a leak path at every interface. The concept becomes credible only when every layer has a stable ID, material, thickness, orientation, datum and inspection result. The DXF package must be treated as controlled manufacturing data, not as a collection of similar-looking silhouettes.

## 14\. Tool elements, datums and load paths

The mould needs one declared primary datum system. A practical experimental choice is the upper face of the full base plate as datum A, one long external edge or precision key line as datum B and one end face as datum C. Every cavity layer, core guide and inspection drawing then refers back to A–B–C. Without that convention, each plate can be individually within tolerance while the stack produces a curved cavity or misaligned cores.

Clamp load should travel through broad steel contact, not through a thin cavity web or a protruding core. Washers and a stiff upper plate distribute bolt force. Alignment keys take in-plane shear during assembly but should not be asked to resist all thermal pressure. The cores require positive axial control so they cannot float upward during filling, yet the retention method must release without bending the hot part. A separate mechanical stop or guide plate is preferable to improvised hammering against core ends.

| Load or motion | Intended path | Failure if path is wrong |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Bolt preload | Bolt → washer → clamp plate → full plate stack → base | Local bow, open seams and variable block height |
| Cavity pressure | Polymer → side layers → keyed plate stack → bolts/base | Flash, layer separation or permanent side distortion |
| Core shrink contact | Polymer → polished core → guide/retainer | Bent core, torn web or uncontrolled hot part motion |
| Demould pull | Individual core pull along its own axis | Compound extraction load and fracture if all cores are tied together |
| Handling | Lift points on cold tool frame or full plates | Burn, pinch or misalignment when lifted by a core or bolt |

## 15\. Alignment is not clamping

A bolt through an oversized clearance hole can pull two plates together but cannot repeat their lateral position accurately. If the bolts are also used as locating pins, tightening order changes the cavity, the cores bind and the measured block width becomes an assembly artifact. Dedicated dowels, keys or two deliberately close-fitting reference holes should establish position before any clamp force is applied. The remaining bolt holes should retain enough clearance for thermal movement and straightforward assembly.

The cold proof is successful when a technician can assemble the complete dry stack by hand, insert every core without driving it, tighten the bolts in a written sequence, reopen the tool and reassemble it to the same gauge readings. Witness marks must show the orientation of every asymmetric layer. A layer that can be installed upside down and still appear plausible is a design error; it needs an unmistakable key, mark or geometry change.

**Acceptance boundary.** No polymer enters the mould until the cold stack closes without force, all cores pass through their guides, the cavity can be probed end to end, and three complete assembly–disassembly cycles return the same reference dimensions.

## 16\. Tool progression: mock-up, three-core coupon, full small mould

The tool programme is split into three steps because the main unknown is release, not the artistic completeness of a full block. Stage A is a cold plate-stack mock-up made from inexpensive sheet or the intended steel without heating. It verifies filenames, layer order, keys, bolt access, cavity continuity and core straightness. Stage B is a narrow three-core coupon. It exposes fill, venting, shrink lock and warm extraction with a much smaller material charge and lower stored heat. Stage C is the complete small block tool only after Stage B has produced repeatable records.

| Stage | Built object | Question answered | Exit criterion |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| A · cold geometry | Complete dry stack, no hot polymer | Can the tool assemble, align and reopen repeatably? | Three clean cycles; gauge readings within the provisional stack tolerance |
| B · three-core coupon | Short cavity with three representative cores | Can the selected material fill, vent and release without tearing? | At least five logged coupons with bounded extraction behaviour and interpretable defects |
| C · full small tool | One complete same-revision block cavity | Does the method scale to all cores and full side faces? | Ten-block lot with traceable process and dimensional results |

A failed coupon is useful if its failure is preserved. The part, material label, core order, temperature readings, elapsed times and photographs remain together. The next run changes one principal variable. Changing feedstock, fill mass, heat history, lubricant, core finish and release timing simultaneously would produce another object but not knowledge.

## 17\. Feedstock identity and the first material boundary

The first hot trials must use one known polymer family from one documented lot. ‘Recycled plastic’ is not a material specification. Polymer identity, source, colour, previous use, visible contamination, washing method, drying method, particle-size range and retained sample belong to the batch record. Where identification is uncertain, the material is quarantined or tested before heating. Unknown mixtures and possible PVC contamination are stop conditions because incompatible polymers can separate, degrade or release hazardous products.

The archive does not freeze polypropylene, polyethylene or another polymer for V1, so this book does not invent a processing temperature. A competent material-specific assessment must establish the safe temperature window, ventilation, residence time and degradation indicators for the selected lot. The equipment setting is not the part temperature. At least two independent measurements—such as controlled hot-plate temperature and a protected tool or material probe—are required before temperature is used as a comparison variable.

| Batch field | Required entry | Reason |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Batch ID | Unique human-readable code | Connects every specimen and reject to one lot |
| Declared polymer | Known family and identification method | Prevents a mixed-waste assumption |
| Source | Supplier, collection stream or previous product | Provides contamination context |
| Preparation | Sort, wash, rinse, dry and shred record | Explains moisture and debris |
| Particle range | Screen or measured minimum/maximum | Controls packing and heating repeatability |
| Retained sample | Sealed labelled reference portion | Permits later comparison or analysis |
| Disposition | Released, research only or quarantined | Stops accidental reuse of uncertain material |

## 18\. Sorting, washing, drying and size preparation

Sorting starts before washing. Foreign metal, glass, wood, paper, elastomer, multilayer film, batteries, electronic parts and any container with an unknown hazardous history are removed. The purpose is not cosmetic purity but a controlled feed stream. Washing removes surface dirt and soluble residues using a documented method appropriate to the known polymer and previous service. Rinsing continues until the chosen cleanliness check is met; visual clarity alone is not sufficient where the source carried oils or chemicals.

Drying is verified by a repeatable method suitable for the polymer rather than by touch. Moisture can create steam, bubbles, incomplete fusion and dangerous ejection from a hot open tool. Prepared material is stored closed and labelled so it cannot reabsorb water or be confused with an untreated lot. Shredded particles are screened into a declared range. Very large pieces bridge narrow webs; excessive fines heat differently, escape through seams and increase contamination exposure.

| Preparation checkpoint | Record | Stop condition |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Incoming inspection | Source, appearance, odour and prohibited objects | Unknown chemical container, battery, PVC suspicion or uncontrolled mix |
| After washing | Method, detergent if any, rinse result and dry mass | Persistent residue, unknown odour or unremovable contamination |
| After drying | Time, controlled environment and verification method | Condensation, inconsistent mass or wet storage |
| After shredding | Screen range, fines fraction and equipment clean-down | Metal fragments, mixed colour from prior batch or uncontrolled dust |
| Before release | Label, retained sample and operator sign-off | Missing identity or preparation record |

## 19\. Hot-work cell and operator protection

Hot polymer work requires a bounded, ventilated area with a stable non-combustible bench, temperature-controlled equipment, guarded hot surfaces, suitable gloves, eye and face protection, clothing that does not melt against the skin, and a clear path away from the tool. Local fire controls and emergency arrangements must be selected for the actual equipment and material by a competent person. The operator never leans over a filled cavity, tests softness with bare skin or uses water on an unknown hot system.

The mould contains pinch points and stores heat after the visible polymer surface has cooled. A simple status marker—COLD, HEATING, HOT / DO NOT TOUCH, WARM RELEASE and SAFE TO HANDLE—prevents a second person from treating the tool as cold scrap. One person directs the cycle. A second trained person may observe and record, but nobody reaches into the closure zone while a bolt, clamp or press can move.

**No recipe without a material.** This edition intentionally omits a universal melt temperature and heating time. Publishing one number for an unidentified waste stream would create false precision and unsafe practice. The controlled material lot and its verified processing window must come first.

## 20\. One complete manufacturing cycle

The cycle begins with records, not heat. The operator opens a numbered run sheet, confirms the current drawing revision, identifies the tool and core set, records the batch ID and verifies that measuring instruments are in service. The clean cold mould is inspected for damage, residue, burrs, blocked vents and loose keys. Each core is checked for straightness and surface damage. Any approved release treatment is identified and applied by a repeatable minimal method; an unnamed workshop oil is not acceptable process data.

The lower package, side halves and cores are assembled against the declared datums. Bolts are brought to contact and tightened in the same crossed sequence. Empty cavity access and vent routes are verified. A weighed charge is prepared, with unused material kept separate. Heating and filling follow the material-specific trial plan. The operator records time, set temperature, measured tool/material temperatures, visible fusion, vent behaviour, additions, compaction actions and any smoke, odour or colour change.

At the planned warm-release condition the clamp is reduced in a controlled order, side breakaway is initiated and cores are extracted individually while their order, force proxy, elapsed time and damage are recorded. The part remains supported flat during cooling. Only at the defined handling condition is it moved to the inspection station. Tool residue and every reject are labelled before the next batch enters the cell.

## 21\. Filling, venting and compaction

A full-sided block with narrow internal webs can trap air even when the top appears full. The fill method must therefore make flow paths and vents observable. The first coupon should use a simple, repeatable charge distribution rather than random handfuls. Material is weighed, divided into planned additions and placed so that one region does not complete long before another. If external pressure or tamping is used, the tool, operator barrier and load path must be designed for it; improvised impact on a hot open cavity is excluded.

Vent routes belong at the last-to-fill regions identified by the coupon, but a vent must not become an uncontrolled flash knife or spray path. The operator records the first appearance of material, gas or moisture at each vent. A blocked vent, unexpected vapour pulse, sharp degradation odour, darkening material or visible separation ends the run. More heat is not the default repair for incomplete fusion; charge distribution, moisture, particle range, venting, surface temperature and pressure are considered separately.

| Observed defect | Possible mechanism | Next controlled check |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Unfilled web or corner | Bridging, cold region, trapped gas or insufficient charge | Map location; verify charge distribution and temperature at that zone |
| Large bubbles | Moisture, entrained air or degradation gas | Stop; verify drying and safe processing window |
| Layer-line flash | Open seam, burr, plate bow or excessive pressure | Cold-check stack and clamp distribution |
| Dark / brittle patch | Overheating, long residence or contamination | Quarantine batch and inspect temperature history |
| Core-to-core variation | Uneven heat, finish, geometry or extraction timing | Record cores individually; do not average the result away |

## 22\. Warm breakaway and individual core release

Core release is the central mechanical experiment. As the polymer cools, it contracts against the long core surfaces. If the tool remains fully closed until the part is cold, shrink pressure and friction may lock several cores together. Pulling the complete core set at once adds the forces and can tear internal webs. The proposed process interrupts that lock while the material is strong enough to retain shape but still warm enough to relax locally.

The side packages are first unloaded according to a defined safe sequence so the body can relieve slightly. Cores are then extracted one at a time along their axes. The same core order is used until a deliberate order experiment is run. Each extraction is classified with a calibrated force gauge when available; until then a declared proxy such as pull-tool reading, standardized tap count or lever displacement is recorded. ‘Easy’ and ‘hard’ are insufficient unless the operator scale is defined before the run.

| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Core ID and position | A1, A2, A3… with diagram orientation | Separates geometry and temperature position effects |
| Release time | Elapsed time from heating stop / tool opening | Connects extraction to cooling state |
| Temperature | Defined surface/tool reading and instrument | Makes ‘warm’ reproducible |
| Force or proxy | Peak force, standardized tap count or other predeclared scale | Reveals shrink-lock growth and surface damage |
| Motion quality | Clean, stick–slip, rotation, bending or seizure | Distinguishes friction from alignment failure |
| Damage | Core scoring, web tear, whitening, deformation or residue | Shows whether apparent release consumed part life |

## 23\. The three-core extraction experiment

The coupon contains an edge core, a centre core and a second edge or otherwise thermally distinct core. Its outer wall and minimum web should represent the intended V1 difficulty rather than an unrealistically thick test bar. Thermocouples or protected contact measurements are placed so they do not create a new release mechanism. The coupon has enough length to show axial friction but remains small enough to stop safely and section after a failed run.

A baseline series holds geometry, material lot, charge, core finish and heating method constant while varying only release state. A second series may compare an approved surface finish or release treatment, but only after the untreated baseline is understood. At least five interpretable coupons at one condition are needed before a trend is called repeatable. All coupons, including visibly failed ones, receive IDs and dispositions.

| Run family | Controlled variable | Minimum useful observation |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| C0 · cold assembly | No polymer | Core insertion/removal, alignment and gauge repeatability |
| C1 · early warm | Release earlier in cooling | Whether shape collapses or webs smear |
| C2 · mid warm | Intermediate state | Whether cores release with bounded force and intact webs |
| C3 · late warm | Later cooling | Onset and rate of shrink lock |
| C4 · surface comparison | One defined core finish or release system | Measured change without material or geometry change |

## 24\. Cooling, part support and demould handling

A newly released block can be geometrically complete and still be unstable. Unequal cooling between thick side faces, thin webs and locator forms can bow the body or twist the seating planes. The part is placed on a flat, clean support that does not block necessary cooling paths or imprint a soft surface. The support method, orientation, contact material, cooling time and room conditions are recorded. Fans, water, clamping weights or forced straightening are not added silently.

The earliest handling time is defined by a repeatable condition, not by impatience. If the block deforms under its own mass or a light prescribed gauge, it remains supported. A warped part is not reheated and pressed flat before inspection, because that would erase evidence about the tool and cycle. After as-moulded measurements and photographs, a separate labelled research experiment may examine correction or annealing.

## 25\. Tool cleaning, service and lifecycle record

After every cycle the tool cools to the declared safe-cleaning state. Polymer residue is removed with methods that do not round core corners, scratch sealing faces or leave an unidentified coating. Every plate and core is inspected under consistent light. Damage is mapped by component ID. A core that was polished or reworked receives a new service entry because its friction condition may have changed even if its nominal across-flats measurement did not.

| Service item | Routine check | Escalation threshold |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Layer faces | Residue, burr, corrosion, bow and witness-mark clarity | Any step into cavity, open seam or lost orientation mark |
| Cores | Across flats, straightness, scoring, end damage and residue | Out-of-limit size, bending, seizure mark or chipped corner |
| Keys / dowels | Free insertion and visible wear | Rocking stack, forced fit or changed gauge result |
| Bolts / washers | Thread, bearing surface and equal engagement | Galling, stretched thread, cracked washer or uneven clamp |
| Vents | Clear passage and stable geometry | Blocked route or enlarged flash path |
| Tool record | Cycle count, event and disposition | Missing history or unapproved repair |

# PART IV · QUALITY, TRACEABILITY AND LOCAL PRODUCTION

## 26\. Block inspection: identity before performance

Inspection begins before a dimension is taken. The part ID, geometry revision, mould/core set, material batch, run number, operator, date and disposition must agree with the cycle record. An attractive object without that chain is an anonymous sample, not evidence for repeatability. The as-moulded block is photographed from six orthogonal directions and at least one oblique direction beside a scale and identity card before trimming, sanding, reheating or fitting.

The first inspection is deliberately non-destructive. Mass is recorded on a verified balance. Overall length, width and height are measured at declared locations. Seating faces are checked on a reference plane for rock, bow and twist. Every void is illuminated and passed with a straightness probe. Minimum visible web, side faces, corners, locator forms, pockets, flash, cold joints, bubbles, inclusions, sink, scorch and release damage are mapped. A passing appearance cannot override a blocked void or missing identity.

## 27\. Ten-block same-revision repeatability proof

One good block shows possibility. Ten consecutive blocks from the same geometry revision and declared material lot begin to show process stability. The lot is not cherry-picked. Every started cycle receives a run number, including aborts and obvious failures. If the tool is repaired, a material lot is changed or a core finish is altered, the intervention is recorded and the sequence may become a new lot rather than silently continuing the old one.

The ten-block proof asks four questions. Can the tool cycle without accumulating damage? Can the block remain within provisional mass and dimensional bands? Can the locators assemble repeatedly without hand correction? Do defects and extraction forces stay bounded instead of drifting with tool temperature or residue? Statistical claims are premature with ten parts, but the raw sequence, range, median and run-order plot can reveal drift and special causes.

| Proof item | Lot rule | Failure signal |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Completeness | All ten starts and aborts are retained in the log | Missing or discarded run |
| Identity | One frozen CAD/DXF revision and one declared material lot | Unrecorded geometry, feedstock or tool change |
| Mass / envelope | Same methods and locations for every block | Trend, step change or unexplained outlier |
| Core release | Each core remains separately recorded | Rising force, repeated seizure or position-specific tearing |
| Assembly | At least two standard neighbours and ten seat/unseat cycles | Filing, heating, fracture, excessive rocking or retained deformation |
| Tool state | Cold inspection before and service record after each run | Progressive flash, bow, scoring or alignment loss |

**Minimum proof.** The first credible process milestone is not ‘a block came out’. It is a complete ten-start sequence whose successful and failed parts, tool interventions and measurements can all be reconstructed.

## 28\. Human-readable marking and traceability

The first trace system remains readable without a phone, cloud account or proprietary database. A moulded or permanently applied revision mark identifies the geometry. A compact batch/run code identifies the material and cycle. The external log expands that code into dates, source, tool components and measurements. A later QR or digital passport may duplicate the record, but it cannot be the only route to identity on an experimental part.

Marking must not create a thin crack starter, trap water or occupy a locator/gauge surface. The mark location and minimum legibility are frozen in the drawing. If hot marking, engraving, label adhesive or ink is used, its effect on the selected polymer and recycling route is evaluated separately. A part with an unreadable mark can remain a research specimen but cannot enter the wall proof as a traceable pass block.

| Code element | Example structure | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| System | PWS-HC | PWS HexCell family |
| Geometry revision | V1-R00 | Frozen block drawing revision |
| Material batch | B260804-A | Declared prepared feedstock lot |
| Run | R01…R10 | Individual started cycle |

## 29\. Defect disposition, rework and regrind

Disposition preserves the meaning of evidence. PASS means the block meets the current experimental acceptance criteria and may enter the next declared test. RESEARCH ONLY means it remains useful for sectioning, handling or a bounded comparison but cannot be counted as a conforming wall component. REGRIND means the material may re-enter a controlled stream only after the effect of additional thermal history and contamination risk is addressed. QUARANTINE means identity or safety is uncertain and the material cannot return to production.

Rework is not invisible correction. Removing flash may be allowed if the drawing defines a non-functional trim line and both as-moulded and final masses are recorded. Filing a locator, drilling a blocked void, heating a warped face or gluing a crack changes the specimen and disqualifies it from the as-produced repeatability lot. Such operations may form a separate labelled repair study.

| Observed condition | Default disposition | Allowed next use |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Complete, traceable and within provisional criteria | PASS | Assembly cycling and wall proof after lot review |
| Traceable cosmetic defect outside gauge surfaces | RESEARCH ONLY unless criterion explicitly permits | Surface study or non-counted demonstration |
| Blocked void, torn web, major warp or modified locator | RESEARCH ONLY | Sectioning and root-cause investigation |
| Known compatible clean offcut / reject | REGRIND candidate | Controlled reprocessing study with thermal-history record |
| Unknown batch, suspect PVC, foreign object or degradation | QUARANTINE | Competent identification or safe disposal route |

## 30\. The local one-way microfactory cell

The first production cell is arranged so dirty incoming material cannot cross back into clean prepared feedstock or finished inspection. The sequence is receipt and quarantine, sorting, washing, drying, shredding/screening, closed prepared storage, weighed charge, hot tool, warm release, flat cooling, inspection, disposition and records. Regrind returns through a separately controlled route. Airflow, drainage, dust collection, noise and hot-work controls must fit the local equipment and regulations.

A small cell succeeds by discipline rather than throughput. Each location has a clear status, container ID and clean-down boundary. Tools that contact unknown incoming waste do not touch dried feedstock. The hot-work zone cannot be a general storage surface. Inspection remains physically downstream so a rejected block cannot be mistaken for a released block. A simple board showing the active geometry revision, batch and run prevents parallel undocumented experiments.

| Zone | Permitted material state | Separation rule |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Receipt / quarantine | Unknown or unverified incoming objects | No contact with clean tools or prepared storage |
| Wet preparation | Sorted wash stream | Drainage and residues remain outside the dry zone |
| Dry preparation | Verified dry material and controlled shred | Closed labelled containers; dust capture and equipment clean-down |
| Hot work | One released batch and weighed charge | Bounded access, ventilation and no unrelated storage |
| Cooling | Hot/warm identified parts | Flat supports and status labels; no early handling |
| Inspection / archive | Cooled traceable parts | PASS, research, regrind and quarantine physically separated |

# PART V · FIRST 1 m² WALL PROOF

## 31\. HexCell inside the wider PWS architecture

HexCell is the repeating material element, not the complete building envelope. The wider PWS idea includes restraint, seals, drainage, removable faces, service access and later specialised layers. V1 keeps those interfaces external wherever possible so a weakness in one block is not confused with a façade attachment, vacuum system or electronic feature. The first wall is therefore a transparent test rig around one block type, not a miniature certified house.

The block carries three immediate duties: remain handleable, repeat course position and expose its internal condition. The rig carries global stability and prevents the experimental wall from falling. Threaded rods and a top beam apply bounded vertical compression. Dry seals and at most two pressure zones test contact behaviour. A removable face may be attached to the independent frame, not claimed as a proven load path through untested polymer.

| Subsystem | First-wall duty | Explicitly not claimed |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| HexCell blocks | Repeatable dry courses and visible cellular bodies | Certified structural masonry, insulation or fire barrier |
| Independent frame | Prevent collapse and define test geometry | Evidence that the blocks alone are self-supporting |
| Threaded rods / top beam | Apply and retain measured preload | A final architectural connection detail |
| Dry seals | Bounded contact and leakage experiment | Permanent weatherproof envelope |
| Removable face | Explore serviceable attachment to the rig | Approved façade or cladding system |
| Drainage base | Collect and measure introduced water | Long-term moisture durability |

## 32\. Configuration of the first 1 m² rig

The target is approximately one square metre of exposed wall area using one frozen block revision. Exact course count follows the measured block and joint geometry. The rig stands on a level base with an independent frame on both ends, a lower drainage tray, two vertical threaded rods outside or in deliberately isolated routes, a load-spreading top beam, guarded restraint against overturning and a removable test face where required. The assembly remains accessible from both sides.

Blocks are placed in staggered courses with a documented start/end arrangement. Special corner mouldings are not introduced. Any cut block, spacer or end restraint is clearly nonstandard and excluded from the repeated-block statistics. The wall is not placed above people, used as a guard, loaded as a roof support or connected to an occupied building. Its exclusion zone remains in force during impact, pressure, heat and water tests.

## 33\. Dry assembly sequence

### 33.1 Base and first course

The empty rig is photographed and measured. The base datum and wall centreline are marked. Blocks are selected from the accepted ten-block lot without hiding the highest or lowest measured parts. The first course is placed dry against a known straight reference. No adhesive, foam, heating or hand carving is permitted. Each block ID, orientation and seat condition is entered on the wall map.

### 33.2 Staggered courses

The second course is offset by the frozen overlap and lowered so both locators enter visible receiving pockets without side impact. The operator records seating force or a predeclared hand scale, gap, rock and correction. Subsequent courses repeat the pattern while cumulative line, level, face offset and course height are measured. If error accumulates, the wall is stopped and mapped rather than forced straight by the top beam.

### 33.3 Seals, rods and top beam

Only after the uncompressed dry wall is recorded are the selected seals installed in their declared interfaces. Rods and the top beam are fitted without preload and checked for free alignment. Preload is applied in small equal increments while top-beam displacement, wall width, local gaps and block sounds or whitening are observed. Torque alone is a poor force measurement; if torque is used as a proxy, thread condition, lubrication, washer and calibration procedure remain fixed and are disclosed.

### 33.4 Baseline hold

The completed wall rests for a declared period before dynamic tests. Rod force or proxy, top-beam movement, course line, wall bow, temperature and photographs are repeated. A sudden loss of preload, growing bow, locator damage, creeping gap or audible cracking stops the programme and triggers safe unloading.

## 34\. Vertical preload and restraint

Vertical compression can improve contact and prevent blocks from lifting, but too much preload can buckle thin faces, crush webs or hide poor locator fit. The test therefore begins near zero and increases by declared increments. The load path is top beam to broad bearing interface, through the stacked seating planes, to the level base. Rods react the load through the independent rig. No concentrated nut or washer bears directly on a thin polymer face.

Preload is bounded by observed deformation and the unknown capacity of the experimental blocks, not by an arbitrary construction value. Measurements include total force or calibrated proxy, loaded area, top-beam displacement, wall shortening, face bow and local damage. If the rods are tightened unequally, the wall can be forced into a misleading straightness or twist; left and right readings are therefore recorded separately at each increment.

| Preload state | Required observations | Stop condition |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| P0 · contact | Beam seated; minimal force; baseline wall geometry | Any block already requires force to enter position |
| P1 · low | Left/right force proxy, shortening, gaps and sound | Local whitening, cracking or sudden movement |
| P2 · working trial | Repeated readings after hold | Progressive creep or unacceptable seal/face distortion |
| P3 · bounded exploration | Only after prior states are stable and risk review permits | Any fixture or block behaviour outside the predeclared envelope |
| Unload | Recovery, permanent set and damage map | Unsafe stored movement; use guarded release procedure |

## 35\. Dry seals and limited pressure zones

A seal experiment is useful only if the leakage path can be located. The first wall uses no more than two controlled pressure zones, each with a known boundary and accessible seal. This prevents a large network of cells from producing an attractive pressure reading that cannot be diagnosed. Seals remain removable and are identified by material, section, lot, compression target and placement. Adhesive caulk is excluded from the baseline because it would mask geometry and prevent clean disassembly.

The pressure test remains small and guarded. The enclosed volume, pressure instrument, connection, relief path and maximum differential pressure are documented. The operator approaches the pressure in increments and observes both pressure decay and physical movement. A successful decay curve is not an insulation or wind-load certificate. It is evidence about one rig, seal condition, temperature and time window.

| Seal variable | Record | Interpretation limit |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Material / profile | Supplier or source, dimensions, lot and condition | No generic claim about all elastomers or foams |
| Compression | Initial thickness, installed gap and calculated compression | Gap accuracy and material creep remain sources of error |
| Zone volume | Measured or bounded volume including tubing | Needed to compare decay between setups |
| Pressure | Initial differential, time series and temperature | Small rig result only; not a façade rating |
| Leak location | Smoke-free safe tracer, sound, local probe or section isolation | A global curve cannot locate a path by itself |
| Reuse | Seal condition after disassembly and second assembly | One seal cycle does not establish service life |

## 36\. Water entry, drainage and moisture observation

The water test introduces a measured spray from one controlled side while the opposite side, lower tray and internal openings remain observable. Water quantity, spray geometry, distance, duration, pressure, wall temperature and ambient conditions are recorded. The test starts at low severity. Wind-driven rain certification is outside scope; the purpose is to identify direct paths, pooling, capillary retention and whether the base can drain without hiding moisture.

Collected water is weighed or measured by zone. Tracer-safe colouring may be used only if compatible with materials and cleanup. After spray stops, the wall is inspected immediately and at declared intervals. Blocks and seals are weighed where useful. The wall is then disassembled so hidden contact faces can be mapped. A dry outer surface is not proof that no water remains inside a pocket or between courses.

| Observation | Method | Decision supported |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Applied water | Calibrated container or flow/time measurement | Normalises the trial |
| First visible entry | Time-stamped zone photograph | Identifies path onset |
| Collected drainage | Separate zone containers by time | Distinguishes drained from retained water |
| Retained moisture | Mass change and disassembly inspection | Reveals hidden storage or blocked route |
| Damage / swelling | Dimensions, fit and surface map after dry-down | Determines whether exposure changed assembly |

## 37\. Wall test sequence and measurement discipline

The wall programme proceeds from least damaging to more demanding so each stage retains an interpretable baseline. First comes dry assembly and ten-seat cycling of selected interfaces. Second is a preload hold and unload/reload cycle. Third is a bounded lateral push or pendulum/soft-body impact chosen by a competent test plan. Fourth is a heat–cool environmental exposure within the verified material boundary. Fifth is controlled water spray. Sixth is the small pressure-decay experiment. Final disassembly occurs even if earlier results appear successful.

Every stage has a pre-test state, applied input, time series, stop rule and post-test inspection. The rig is never left loaded without status and access control. Damage is not repaired between stages unless the programme deliberately restarts from a documented new baseline. When a result is surprising, the correct response is to preserve the configuration and inspect it, not immediately repeat at greater severity.

| Order | Test | Primary measurement | Gate to continue |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1 | Assembly / reassembly | Seating, gaps, line/level and damage after cycles | No forced fitting or progressive locator failure |
| 2 | Preload hold | Force/proxy, shortening, bow, creep and recovery | Stable bounded response and safe unloading |
| 3 | Lateral push / bounded impact | Input, displacement, residual set and damage map | Rig stable; no uncontrolled fragment or collapse risk |
| 4 | Heat–cool | Temperatures, movement, fit and residual distortion | Material remains within verified safe range |
| 5 | Water spray | Applied, drained and retained water plus paths | No unsafe interaction with equipment or hidden rig instability |
| 6 | Small pressure decay | Pressure, volume, temperature and time | Zone boundaries remain secure |
| 7 | Complete disassembly | Time, force/proxy, damage and recovered parts | All material accounted for and classified |

## 38\. Complete disassembly and reuse proof

Disassembly is a principal experiment, not cleanup. The preload is removed through a guarded, written sequence. The top beam, rods, seals, face and each block are removed and mapped. Time, tools, force proxies, seized interfaces, damage and contamination are recorded. Parts are cleaned only after the as-disassembled state is photographed. Every block returns to inspection and receives a post-wall disposition.

A reuse claim requires a second assembly with identified recovered blocks and seals, not merely a statement that the wall could be rebuilt. The same geometric and pressure checks are repeated at reduced or equal severity. Replaced items are counted and reasons are listed. The most informative output may be that one cheap external seal is consumable while the blocks remain reusable; the architecture should report that honestly rather than forcing every component into a ‘zero waste’ narrative.

| Recovery class | Definition | Book language |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Reassembled unchanged | Same identified component returns without repair | Direct reuse demonstrated for the tested cycle |
| Cleaned and reassembled | Defined cleaning only; no geometry change | Reuse after recorded maintenance |
| Repaired | Material added, removed, heated or reshaped | Repair experiment; not unchanged reuse |
| Reprocessed | Block converted to controlled feedstock | Material recovery experiment; not component reuse |
| Discarded / quarantined | Unsafe, unknown or uneconomic recovery | Loss is counted and reason preserved |

# PART VI · PHYSICS, FAILURE CONTROL, COST AND BOUNDARIES

## 39\. Screening equations and what they can reveal

Simple equations are useful when they expose a controlling scale and remain attached to measured inputs. They are dangerous when an unknown coefficient is selected to make a finished-looking result. The relations below are therefore screening models. They help design coupons and instruments; they do not predict a certified block or wall. Every calculation sheet must list its source dimensions, units, measurement condition and uncertainty.

### 39.1 Geometric shrinkage

For a reference length L and measured free linear shrinkage strain ε, the dimensional change is ΔL \= εL. A 100 mm trial bar that becomes 99.0 mm has a measured shrinkage of 1%, but the moulded HexCell will not necessarily shrink uniformly. Longitudinal, transverse and thickness directions can differ because of flow, cooling and constraint. The correct V1 correction is derived from same-material coupons and actual cavity measurements, not a generic internet shrinkage number.

### 39.2 Thermal movement

For a stable temperature range and a measured or reliable material coefficient α, free thermal movement is approximated by ΔL \= αLΔT. The wall concern is not only one block changing length. Unequal sun exposure, dark surfaces, steel restraints, dry seals and polymer courses can move differently. A test rig should therefore measure temperature at more than one location and allow the distinction between free movement, restrained force and permanent creep.

### 39.3 Core extraction

A first friction model is F ≈ μpA, where μ is an effective friction coefficient, p is average shrink contact pressure and A is polymer–core contact area. For MOLD-REF-7, the seven reference cores present roughly 0.011 m² of contact. Neither μ nor p is known. The equation nevertheless explains why individual extraction, warm release, polished straight cores and shorter representative coupons are rational. The measured force-versus-time curve is more valuable than a guessed coefficient.

### 39.4 Pressure force and preload stress

A pressure difference Δp acting over zone area A produces a resultant F \= ΔpA that must be reacted by the independent rig. Even a small pressure across a large panel can create a significant force, so the wall test remains low-pressure and guarded. Average compressive stress under vertical preload is σ \= P/Ab, where P is measured preload and Ab is the effective bearing area. Local webs and imperfect contact may see much higher stress than this average; visual and displacement measurements remain essential.

### 39.5 Pressure decay

For a fixed test volume and approximately stable temperature, a falling differential pressure indicates leakage and/or volume relaxation. A simple comparison may use the time to fall between two declared pressure levels or a fitted decay constant. Temperature drift, seal creep, flexible walls, instrument resolution and connection leakage can dominate the result. The empty fixture and tubing must be checked before the wall is credited with a curve.

| Model | Needed measured inputs | Useful decision | Not established |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| ΔL \= εL | Coupon and block dimensions before/after controlled condition | CAD allowance and locator clearance | Universal polymer shrinkage |
| ΔL \= αLΔT | Temperature field, length and material coefficient | Rig movement allowance and seal trial range | Long-term façade movement |
| F ≈ μpA | Core area plus measured extraction force/time/temperature | Coupon length, release window and surface comparison | Exact friction or contact pressure |
| F \= ΔpA | Zone pressure and projected area | Frame and restraint sizing for the bounded test | Wind resistance or code compliance |
| σ \= P/Ab | Preload and effective bearing geometry | Compare preload states and visible deformation | Local web stress or compressive strength |
| pressure decay | Volume, pressure, time and temperature | Compare two controlled seal assemblies | Airtightness certification |

## 40\. Principal failure modes and control logic

Failure analysis starts with the entire evidence chain. A dimensional block can fail because the material was mixed. A good material can fail because one vent closed. A complete part can fail during late core extraction. Ten blocks can pass individually yet accumulate course error. A wall can hold pressure because adhesive accidentally sealed the diagnostic joint. Controls are placed before, during and after each mechanism so the result does not depend on final appearance alone.

| Failure mode | Effect | Early indicator | Primary prevention / response |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Unknown or mixed feedstock | Incompatible fusion, degradation, emissions and untraceable results | Uncertain source, odour, separation or inconsistent melt behaviour | Quarantine; identify one polymer family; retain a sample |
| Residual moisture | Bubbles, steam event, weak webs | Mass instability, condensation or vent pulse | Verified drying and closed storage; stop hot run |
| Layer misalignment | Stepped cavity, core bind and flash | Cold gauge drift or forced core insertion | Dedicated keys, datums and three dry cycles |
| Blocked vent / bridging | Incomplete webs and trapped gas | Uneven fill, late gas release or void map | Three-core coupon, particle control and observable vents |
| Overheating / long residence | Brittleness, colour change and hazardous decomposition | Darkening, smoke or sharp odour | Material-specific boundary, multiple temperature measurements and immediate stop |
| Shrink lock | Seized cores and torn internal webs | Rising release proxy, stick–slip and scoring | Warm breakaway, individual cores and timed extraction study |

| Uneven cooling | Bow, twist and locator misfit | Growing plane gap during cooling | Defined flat support; record before any correction |
| Locator tolerance failure | Forced assembly or excessive rocking | High seat force, filing or persistent gap | Freeze measured clearance; ten-cycle mating test |
| Course accumulation | Wall lean, open joints and false preload | Line/level drift by course | Map every block and stop before force correction |
| Excess preload | Web crushing, face buckling and creep | Whitening, sound, rapid shortening or force loss | Incremental measured load and guarded unloading |
| Hidden water retention | Contamination, freeze/biological risk and misleading dry face | Mass gain or wet contact after disassembly | Drainage tray, zone collection and post-test opening |
| Record loss | No defensible result or reuse identity | Unreadable mark, missing run or changed revision | Human-readable IDs, protected records and physical separation |

A formal FMEA score can be added after the team defines consistent severity, occurrence and detection scales. Multiplying arbitrary scores in the first meeting creates false rankings. Until then, any hazard involving unknown fumes, hot ejection, uncontrolled pressure, collapse, fire or uncertain material identity is treated as a stop condition regardless of a numerical priority score.

## 41\. First-tool cost and resource envelope

The recovered archive carried a rough experimental-tool estimate of EUR 250–700, with a healthier working target of roughly EUR 350–500 for the small layered mould. These are historical planning numbers, not supplier quotations and not current market prices. They exclude development labour, failed cuts, heating equipment, ventilation, safety controls, measurement instruments, feedstock preparation, wall rig, certification and production scaling.

The cost structure matters more than the total. Laser-cut plate cost grows with material thickness, cut length, hole quality and post-processing. Cores may dominate if straight hex bar is unavailable and precision fabrication is required. Alignment components and a stiff clamp plate should not be removed to save money because they determine whether the cavity can be compared between runs. A staged purchase prevents the full tool from consuming the budget before the three-core release question is answered.

| Cost group | Historical / planning basis | Decision rule |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Cold mock-up | Low-cost sheet, sample steel or partial plate set | Buy only enough to validate stack, keys and file topology |
| Three-core coupon | Representative steel, three cores, basic clamp and instruments | Fund before full cavity; preserve reusable parts |
| Full small mould | Archive estimate EUR 250–700; target EUR 350–500 | Request current quotations against a frozen DXF pack |
| Hot-work cell | Not included in mould estimate | Do not start without material-specific ventilation and protection |
| Inspection | Not included | Prefer simple calibrated gauges and traceable records over cosmetic finishing |
| 1 m² rig | Not included | Independent safety frame and instrumentation are separate work packages |

## 42\. Safety, regulatory and use boundaries

PWS HexCell revision 0.1 is an experimental invention record. It is not a construction product declaration, structural design, fire-rated assembly, pressure vessel, electrical enclosure, food-contact part or instruction to process unidentified waste. The green print is not evidence of recycled content. The reference calculations are not certification. The proposed wall must remain non-occupied and independently restrained.

Before any building application, competent laboratories and professionals would need to address material identity and consistency; mechanical strength and creep; connection and impact behaviour; fire reaction, flame spread, smoke and toxic products; thermal and acoustic performance; water, vapour, drainage and biological effects; UV and weathering; emissions and indoor-air suitability; dimensional stability; durability; repair, reuse and end-of-life; and the applicable Croatian, European and project-specific regulatory route. The relevant requirements may change, so they must be checked for the actual jurisdiction and date of application.

**Use boundary.** Do not use an untested PWS HexCell block in a load-bearing, overhead, occupied, guarding, fire-separating or weather-critical position. All hot-work, pressure, impact and wall experiments require competent local risk controls and an independent safety structure.

A freeze is not permanent dogma. It is a controlled baseline. What is prohibited is a silent edit that leaves photographs, DXFs and test sheets carrying one unchanged name.

## 47\. Two materially different later branches

Two branches are preserved because they change the system's purpose, not merely its colour or scale. Neither branch modifies the V1 tool package. They begin from measured, traceable HexCell parts and receive independent IDs, drawings and test plans. If a branch needs side hooks, embedded parts or a different void pattern, it becomes a new block revision rather than an undocumented option in the V1 mould.

### 47.1 PWS-STEM-01: small educational construction set

The educational branch uses small, nonstructural blocks to make material identity, cellular geometry, staggered courses, dry assembly and disassembly directly visible. Parts may be 3D printed or produced by another safe, age-appropriate process; they are not represented as recycled-polymer construction blocks unless the material is actually documented. The set avoids heating, sharp tools, pressurised cells, fragile snap features and hidden magnets for young users. A flat base, a limited number of identical blocks, two end restraints and illustrated build cards are sufficient for the first exercises.

A useful lesson begins by weighing a solid reference prism and a HexCell form of the same outer envelope, then maps the missing volume without claiming strength. A second exercise compares aligned and staggered joints under a small safe lateral push. A third assembles, photographs, disassembles and sorts every component for reuse. The learning record distinguishes observation from conclusion: learners can report mass, count, fit and recoverability, but cannot infer fire safety, structural capacity or ecological benefit from appearance.

| STEM component | First specification | Safety / evidence boundary |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Blocks | One visible geometry, rounded handling edges and durable IDs | No building-use or recycled-content claim without evidence |
| Base / ends | Stable low-height tabletop restraint | Prevents collapse without implying block self-support |
| Build cards | Mass/void, course staggering and reuse exercises | No hot processing or hazardous feedstock handling |
| Record sheet | Count, mass, photographs, failed fits and recovered parts | Observation is kept separate from engineering certification |
| Acceptance | Ten assembly cycles without fragments, sharp damage or lost identity | Age suitability and local product-safety requirements still need review |

### 47.2 PWS-FUTURE-01: serviceable wall-envelope branch

The later envelope branch returns to the larger PWS aim: wall layers that can be accessed, diagnosed, replaced and materially separated. A future assembly may combine a dry block field with independent internal and external faces, controlled seals, drainage, service channels, replaceable insulation cassettes and, only after simpler tests, small monitored or reduced-pressure cells. Each function remains separable so a failed seal does not require destroying the block and a new sensor does not force a new load-bearing geometry.

This branch is intentionally downstream of the one-square-metre wall. Pressure zones are limited and serviceable; they are not vacuum-insulated building panels by assumption. Sensors may record temperature, humidity, pressure and leak location, but the wall must remain inspectable when electronics fail. External cladding and internal finish attach to an independent rail or tested interface. Fire barriers, cavity stops, vapour control, drainage and structural restraint require competent project-specific design and cannot be inherited from the bare HexCell form.

| Future layer or function | Reason to keep it separate | Evidence required before integration |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Removable inner face | Provides service access without cutting blocks | Repeated removal, attachment load, fire and indoor-air review |
| Removable outer face | Allows weather layer replacement | Wind, impact, drainage, UV, fire and safe-failure testing |
| Service route | Keeps cables/pipes inspectable | Temperature, condensation, separation and code-compliant detailing |
| Insulation cassette | Changes thermal layer without changing block | Thermal, moisture, fire and dimensional compatibility |
| Limited pressure cell | Makes leakage observable and potentially reduces convection | Safe pressure boundary, decay repeatability and graceful loss of pressure |
| Sensors / diagnostics | Locate changes before destructive inspection | Calibration, power loss, privacy, replacement and offline operation |

The gate into either later branch is the same: a measured V1, a repeatable material/tool cycle and a wall that has been completely taken apart. Until then, the branches remain preserved system definitions—not features to be added to the first block.

## Closing technical state

PWS HexCell currently has a clear nucleus, an existing tactile print, a bounded historical reference geometry, an experimental layered-tool architecture and a complete route to repeatable evidence. Its strongest design decision is subtraction. The next edition should not become longer because more imagined features are added; it should become stronger because the green print is measured, V1 is frozen, cores are pulled under recorded conditions, ten honest starts are completed and a one-square-metre wall is assembled and taken apart.

Until those records exist, this book preserves exactly where the invention stands: more than an idea, less than a construction product, and ready for the next controlled physical proof.
